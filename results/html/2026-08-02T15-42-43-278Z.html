Summary

On two benchmark tasks, g-mesh and baseline approaches (grep/Read/Glob) achieved identical accuracy, with both reaching an 83% oracle pass rate. Token efficiency, however, diverged sharply depending on analysis method.

Unconditionally, gmesh consumed 21.8% more tokens than baseline (139,297 vs 114,340 mean tokens). This counterintuitive result contradicts its paired performance: on the five runs where both tools passed oracle checks, gmesh used 62% fewer tokens. The observations attribute this divergence to oracle failures skewing the unconditional comparison—likely because one arm's failing runs consumed disproportionately high token counts.

The benchmark recommends trusting the paired figure (62% reduction) over the unconditional one as more representative of gmesh's actual efficiency. However, gmesh showed no correlation with declared expected winners, suggesting its advantages may be task-dependent rather than universal.

A secondary finding examined whether re-verifying gmesh results with grep/Read undermines its efficiency gains. Results showed that trusting gmesh without additional verification incurred only 1.5% more tokens while improving oracle accuracy from 83% to 100% (6/6 pass rate). This suggests that cautious self-verification practices may be masking gmesh's true cost-benefit advantage in real workflows, where verification overhead can offset token savings.
