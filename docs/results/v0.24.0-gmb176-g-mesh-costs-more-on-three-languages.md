**The untagged row (48 tasks / 220 pairs / -17.8%) did not reproduce here, and
the cause has since been found.** Running `npx tsx harness/report.ts` fresh in

— both computed entirely from this run's own 162 records, since GMB-169's

row draws only on *historical* records from other corpora, and in this worktree

The reason is **data volume, not corpus content**. The untagged row aggregates
every historical record the tree can see, and the two trees do not see the same
number of them: the main checkout holds **156** files under
`results/token-economy/`, this worktree **91** — the difference being historical
result files that were never committed on any branch. Copying the 65 missing
files in and re-running turns the row into exactly `48 / 220 / -17.8%`, and
leaves the semantic and structural rows untouched at `8 / 21 / -16.2%` and
`19 / 54 / -48.8%`. That is a control, not an argument: the figure is a function
of how much history is on disk.

An earlier draft of this document attributed the gap to
`corpora/excalidraw/tasks.json` differing between the trees. That was wrong —
the two copies are byte-identical (`diff -q` reports no difference). The
explanation is recorded here rather than quietly deleted, because the wrong
cause was plausible and someone will reach for it again.

**The consequence worth carrying forward:** any figure drawn from the
accumulated `results/` history is reproducible only against the same set of
files, and that set is currently partly uncommitted. The semantic and structural
rows are immune — they read this run's own records — but the untagged row is
not, and neither is anything else computed over history.

Everything else — separation counts, the single g-mesh win, both correctness
tables, the semantic/structural per-tier savings, the n=1/n=2 split, and the
hash-marking of the six changed tasks — reproduced exactly.

## The headline

On these three corpora, **g-mesh-configured costs more tokens than plain
Read/Grep/Glob.** Range-separated on 15 of 27 tasks — all 15 in baseline's
favor — within noise on 11, and separated in g-mesh's favor on exactly 1.
Summed per repetition, gmesh's total across all 27 tasks is 2,875,847–
2,971,458 tokens (n=3); baseline's is 2,147,817–2,258,145 (n=3) — the ranges
do not overlap on a single repetition, in either tier, in any of the three
corpora (see the per-corpus and per-tier tables below).

**At the same time, g-mesh-configured is slightly more correct and takes
fewer turns.** 79/81 oracle passes against baseline's 77/81, and turns run
lower in both tiers (semantic 8.5 vs 10.3, structural 4.3 vs 5.2). Both
halves belong in the headline together: this is not "g-mesh loses" or
"g-mesh wins," it is g-mesh trading tokens for turns and for two extra
correct answers, and losing that trade on raw token count everywhere except
one task that needed exactly the capability g-mesh adds.

## What can and cannot be compared against GMB-169 (3.8.0)

Three separate reasons a task is not comparable, listed rather than diffed
against a number that was never a fair sample:

**1. Single-run prior (42 of 46 pairs).** Reproduced above: across GMB-169's
four result files, 23 tasks × 2 arms = 46 (task, arm) pairs, of which 42 have
exactly one repetition and 4 have two (`rs-deps-json-printer-workspace-crates`
and `rs-outline-sink-vs-lines`, both arms — the GMB-174 oracle-fix reruns).
n=1 vs n=3 is not a comparison this project trusts for token numbers; none is
attempted here. **For the semantic tier specifically, only 2 of this run's 8
tasks have any GMB-169 prior at all** (`gin-scenario-abort-callers`,
`py-scenario-callers-httpadapter-send`) — both at n=1 — so the semantic tier
as a whole has no usable baseline to diff against; it gets the n=3 baseline
this run establishes, not a delta.

**2. Four tasks are new**, confirmed absent from every GMB-169 record:
`gin-callers-writeheadernow-dispatch`, `py-callers-prepare-two-classes`,
`py-callers-register-hook-mixin`, `rs-callers-flag-name-long-dyn`. There is
nothing to diff against.

**3. Two tasks are edited**, and `computeTaskDefHash` says so rather than
this document asserting it: `gin-find-impl-render`'s GMB-169 record carries
hash `648abef67110`; this run's records carry `c76dbc67db79`. Same for
`rs-callers-sink-matched`: `631e162ae623` → `e78b4231330f`. Both differ,
confirming the def changed (GMB-180 rewrote both tasks' anchors and, for
`gin-find-impl-render`, its oracle mode). Independently, `npx tsx
harness/report.ts` — run fresh in this worktree, not trusted from the ticket
— reports exactly these two tasks' GMB-169 records (2 runs each, 4 total) as
"excluded as stale — graded against a superseded task definition," with no
other of the 23 GMB-169 tasks appearing in that exclusion list. The
instrument marks the six on its own; nothing here had to be bookkept by hand.

That leaves 21 of GMB-169's 23 tasks structurally unchanged and present in
both runs — but 19 of those 21 sit at n=1 on the 3.8.0 side, so even the
"unchanged" tasks mostly can't carry a trustworthy before/after. This
document reports the 3.10.0 side as a baseline, full stop, rather than
picking through 21 tasks to find the 2 with an n=2 GMB-169 prior and
pretending that's a systematic comparison.

## The two correctness disagreements

**`py-scenario-callers-httpadapter-send` — a real disagreement about the
code, reproduced at n=3.** gmesh 3/3, baseline 0/3 (turns 8/11/7 vs 9/8/11).
The task (`oracle.mode: "judge"`) asks whether `Session.send`'s call to
`adapter.send(...)` is a caller of `HTTPAdapter.send` specifically, given
that `get_adapter()` is annotated `-> BaseAdapter`. The judge's per-rep
reasons on all three baseline runs read the same way: baseline finds
`Session.send()`'s call resolves to `HTTPAdapter.send` (naming "dynamic
dispatch" or "runtime resolution" as why), and every rep also misses the one
call site that *does* statically resolve to `HTTPAdapter.send` —
`HTTPDigestAuth.handle_401`'s `r.connection.send(...)` in `auth.py`, reached
through `Response.connection: HTTPAdapter`. This is the same task and the
same failure mode GMB-169 found at n=1 ("the grep arm answered 'Yes ...
through dynamic dispatch' and the judge rejected it for conflating static
analysis with runtime behaviour") — now confirmed to hold across three
independent reps rather than resting on one run.

**`py-references-httpbasicauth` — a grading artifact, not a real
disagreement.** gmesh 1/3, baseline 3/3. This task uses `oracle.mode: "pool"`
with `candidatePool: ["src/requests/auth.py", "src/requests/models.py"]`,
`minMatches: 2` — both pool strings must appear as unnegated substrings in
the answer. `checkPool`'s negation guard (`harness/lib/oracleCheck.ts`)
treats `"excluding"` as a negation cue and blanks out any pool candidate
mentioned within the same sentence/line as that word. Two of gmesh's three
answers open with "excluding the defining file (`src/requests/auth.py`)" or
"Excluding `src/requests/auth.py` (where it's defined)" — the literal pool
string sits in the same sentence as "excluding," so the guard scores it
*unmatched*, leaving only 1 of 2 pool hits, below `minMatches: 2`, hence
`oraclePassed: false`. The third gmesh rep instead writes "defined in
`src/requests/auth.py:84`" as its own separate sentence, with "excluding"
appearing later in a different sentence next to the *unqualified* `auth.py`
(not the full pool-candidate string) — no negation cue lands on the pool
string, so it passes. Baseline never phrases the exclusion this way and
passes all three. **All six answers (three gmesh, three baseline) agree on
the substantive fact**: `src/requests/models.py` is the only file under
`src/` other than `auth.py` itself that references `HTTPBasicAuth`. This
disagreement is entirely in how the negation guard reads gmesh's own
"excluding the defining file" phrasing, not in what either arm found in the
repository.

## Scoring the ticket's written-down prediction

Quoted verbatim from the description, written before the run:

> most of these fixes change answers that were *well-formed and wrong*, and
> a well-formed wrong answer costs an agent turns only when it notices.
> Several tasks may not touch the changed paths at all. A null result on
> most tasks with movement on a named few is the expected shape, and is a
> real result. A large across-the-board improvement is the outcome to
> distrust and investigate.

**Right, on the part it was careful about:** no large across-the-board
*improvement* appeared. That specific outcome — the one the prediction
flagged as suspicious — did not happen.

**Wrong on "a null result on most tasks."** 16 of 27 tasks (59%) were
range-separated, not within noise — a clear majority showed real movement,
not a null result. Only 11 of 27 (41%) were within-noise.

**Wrong in a way the prediction didn't anticipate in either direction: the
across-the-board movement that *did* appear runs *against* g-mesh.** 15 of
the 16 separated tasks moved toward baseline being cheaper — spread across
both tiers, across all three languages, on tasks that have nothing to do
with any of the specific fixes listed in the card (GM-372/373/377/376/378/
382/386/385). The prediction's whole frame was "these named fixes turn wrong
answers right, and that shows up as movement on a named few" — it reasoned
entirely about the *correctness* axis and the specific bugs fixed, and never
considered that the token-cost axis could move broadly on its own, for
reasons unrelated to any of those fixes. It called the direction of a
large across-the-board signal (distrust improvement) without having a model
for the actual across-the-board signal this run produced (regression). A
run that had shown a null result on tokens with correctness moving on a
named few would have matched the prediction. That is not what happened.

**Where the prediction does land correctly: correctness.** Structural-tier
correctness is close to null (55/57 vs 56/57) and semantic-tier correctness
moved in g-mesh's favor (24/24 vs 21/24) — one task,
`py-scenario-callers-httpadapter-send`, accounts for most of that gap and is
exactly a "named few" case. On the correctness axis, read on its own, the
prediction's shape holds up reasonably well. It is the token axis — the
thing this ticket exists to measure — where it was wrong, and wrong in the
specific way of not having anticipated the outcome that occurred.

## Is this the same effect as v0.2.0, or something different?

Checked rather than asserted. `docs/results/v0.2.0-session-economy-findings.md`
found g-mesh costs more than grep on the `lookup` category specifically,
identified the mechanism as `cacheReadTokens` (the per-turn cost of
re-reading gmesh's larger tool schema on every turn of a growing
conversation, which does not amortize) rather than `cacheCreationTokens`
(one-time, and which does amortize), and found the opposite direction on
`ambiguous-name` and `multi-hop`, where gmesh won by margins of +32.8% and
+38.3%.

**The mechanism reproduces exactly.** Averaged across all 162 records:
`cacheReadTokens` is 99,735 (gmesh) vs 73,784 (baseline) — a ~26k gap that is
almost the entire difference between the arms. `cacheCreationTokens` is
7,282 vs 6,216 — a ~1k gap, negligible by comparison. Same asymmetry v0.2.0
found: the persistent, non-amortizing per-turn cost dominates, not the
one-time schema-registration cost.

**But the scope is different, and this run says so rather than papering
over it.** Broken down by this run's own `category` field:

| category | n pairs | gmesh mean | baseline mean | gmesh vs baseline |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| lookup | 39 | 106,264 | 80,486 | +32.0% (costs more) |
| scenario | 18 | 136,042 | 120,733 | +12.7% (costs more) |
| ambiguous-name | 12 | 100,301 | 60,443 | +65.9% (costs more) |
| semantic-search | 9 | 66,802 | 38,732 | +72.5% (costs more) |
| control | 3 | 130,223 | 79,874 | +63.0% (costs more) |

`lookup` costing more matches v0.2.0 directly. **`ambiguous-name` does not**
— v0.2.0's excalidraw corpus found gmesh *winning* there by 32.8%; on gin,
ripgrep and py-requests's three ambiguous-name tasks, gmesh costs 65.9%
*more*. Every category in this run costs more with g-mesh, including the two
categories (`ambiguous-name`, and v0.2.0's `multi-hop`, not present as a
labeled category here but overlapping with what these corpora call
`scenario`) where the earlier finding said the tool's own resolving power
earns back the tax. Caveat, stated the same way GMB-169 stated it for
cross-corpus comparisons: these are three different repositories with
different tasks than v0.2.0's excalidraw corpus, so this category table is
suggestive rather than dispositive on its own. But the direction is
consistent and the mechanism is identical, so the honest read is: **this is
the same effect as v0.2.0 — same cause, same non-amortizing cache-read tax —
extended to three new languages, and extended further than v0.2.0 found it:
here it also dominates on the category that previously offset it.**
