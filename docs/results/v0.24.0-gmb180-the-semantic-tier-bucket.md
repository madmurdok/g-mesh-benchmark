# GMB-180 — building a semantic-tier bucket that can carry a verdict

GMB-175 tagged each of the 23 language tasks with the tier its oracle needs and
found two: gin 1 of 7, ripgrep 0 of 8, py-requests 1 of 8. GMB-176 wants a
per-tier before/after against g-mesh 3.9.0 and 3.10.0, and a two-task bucket
cannot carry one. This is what was added, what was shown about each addition,
and the one thing that turned out to be wrong with the measurement GMB-175
made rather than with the corpora it measured.

## The target: eight tasks, decided before anything was authored

"More than two" is not a target, so here is the arithmetic, from GMB-117's own
numbers over this project's 232 compared task/arm pairs:

| verdict | pairs | share |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `within-noise` (ranges overlap) | 172 | 74.1% |
| `insufficient-n` (both arms n=1 — refused, not judged) | 42 | 18.1% |
| `separated` (ranges do not overlap) | 18 | 7.8% |

Two rates come out of that, and they set the size between them.

**Attrition.** 18.1% of pairs are refused outright at the 1–5 reps this
registry actually runs. At n=2, the chance that at least one of the two tasks
is refused is 1 − (1 − 0.181)² = 33%: one bucket in three reports on a single
task. At n=8 the expected judgeable count is 8 × 0.819 ≈ 6.5, and the chance
of fewer than four surviving is about 2.6%. Eight is the smallest round n at
which the measured refusal rate still leaves a working majority better than
97% of the time.

**Discrimination.** GMB-176 will read this bucket as a count of separated
tasks. Against the background rate — 18 of the 190 judgeable pairs, 9.5% —
a bucket of n tasks produces about 0.095n separations before any real effect
is added. At n=2 the only observation that clears that background is 2 of 2
(p = 0.9%), so the bucket can report "everything moved" or nothing at all: it
has no power against a partial effect, which is the realistic one. At n=8
(≈6.5 judgeable) an observation of ≥3 separated sits at about 3% under the
null. That is the property the two-task bucket does not have, and it is the
actual target: **the smallest n at which a non-unanimous result is still a
finding.**

**What a bucket of eight can and cannot support.**

- *Can*: a bucket-level verdict — "k of the semantic-tier tasks separated,
  against a 9.5% background" — plus the ordinary per-task verdict on each
  task that survived the n=1 refusal.
- *Cannot*: a per-language verdict. Eight tasks over three languages is two
  or three each, which is the two-task problem one level down. GMB-176 should
  report the semantic bucket whole.
- *Cannot*: an effect size. Range-based separation is a yes/no, and at ≤5 reps
  there is no interval to quote.

## The instrument, and two things wrong with it

Everything below was measured with `scripts/probeLanguageTiers.ts`, which runs
a call twice against the same pinned corpus revision: once on this machine's
real PATH, once on a shadow PATH with the language's semantic engine hidden,
each arm in its own `G_MESH_HOME` so no daemon carries a stale PATH across.
GM-382's `provenance.semanticTier == "absent"` — which only the four
edge-walking tools ever carry, and only when a tier that would have mattered is
missing — is the cross-check that the structural arm really ran structural.

Two defects in that instrument had to be fixed first, and both of them had
already produced a wrong tier tag.

### 1. The Rust "structural" arm was never structural

`plugins/rust/plugin.toml` says its semantic command is a bare
`rust-analyzer`, and its own comment says how that is resolved: "a `PATH`
lookup, with `rustup which rust-analyzer` as the fallback". The probe hid
`rust-analyzer` from the shadow PATH and left `rustup` in place. `rustup which
rust-analyzer` answers
`~/.rustup/toolchains/stable-x86_64-apple-darwin/bin/rust-analyzer`, the engine
starts, and both arms are the same arm.

The evidence that this is what happened, rather than a reading of the manifest:
the gin and py-requests structural arms carried
`provenance {"semanticTier":"absent"}` on **every** edge-walking call, and the
ripgrep structural arm carried it on **none** — in either arm. That field is
the daemon saying, per call, that its semantic pass did not contribute; its
absence is the daemon saying the opposite.

So GMB-175's "ripgrep 0 of 8" was not a finding about Rust. It was two
identical arms. `SEMANTIC_ENGINE_BINARY` (one name) is now
`SEMANTIC_ENGINE_BINARIES` (a list), and Rust's is `["rust-analyzer",
"rustup"]`. `cargo` is deliberately left visible: it is a rustup proxy that
dispatches on `argv[0]` and needs no `rustup` on PATH, and the structural
extractor still has to read the workspace.

### 2. A bare ambiguous name never reaches a graph walk

`find_implementations(symbol_name: "Render")` returns twenty declarations that
share the name `Render` — in **both** arms, identically, because the answer
comes off g-mesh's disambiguation rung and no graph walk happens at all. That
is the whole of why `gin-find-impl-render` passed structurally.

The probe now resolves a task's anchor the way g-mesh's own guidance tells a
caller to: `find_definition` first, and when the result is a candidate list,
re-anchor on the candidate declared in the task's own `target.file`. Two rungs
count as a candidate list, not one: `nameAmbiguous`, and `semanticNeighbours` —
the second bites on Rust, where `Flag::name_long` is nobody's `qualifiedName`
(the plugin's is `flags::Flag::name_long`) and the "answer" was three nearest
declarations by meaning.

There is a trap inside that fix, and it cost a wrong reading before it was
caught. Two candidates can sit in the anchor file and mean different things:
gin's *semantic* index carries both `Render` (the interface type) and
`Render.Render` (the interface's own method declaration, which only `go/types`
emits), while the structural index carries only the first. Picking the
highest-ranked candidate in the file therefore resolved the two arms to two
*different* declarations, both of which answered zero — which reads exactly
like "this task is structural". The probe now prefers the candidate whose
`qualifiedName` equals the name the task asked for.

## Oracles

Every expected answer below comes from `scripts/deriveSemanticOracles.ts`,
which reads each corpus's own source at its pinned revision and applies one
stated syntactic rule per task, printing the source line behind each
classification so a reader can check it by eye. No expected answer is taken
from a g-mesh response (GMB-160). Where the two agree — and on gin they agree
exactly, 18 names for 18 names and 10/6 caller sets for 10/6 — that agreement
is a result, not a construction.

## gin — `gin-find-impl-render`, before and after

The task's name says it finds implementations of the `render.Render`
interface. What it measured was that Go's naming convention gives every
implementing method the interface's own name.

**Before.** `oracle.mode: "pool"`, a list of 13 *file paths*, `minMatches: 8`.
The call a bare `symbol_name: "Render"` produces returns, in **both** arms
identically:

```
resolvedBy="nameAmbiguous" ambiguous=true hasMore=true results=20
  Render @ render/render.go          <- the interface itself
  AsciiJSON.Render @ render/json.go
  Context.Render @ context.go        <- not an implementor at all
  ... 17 more
```

Twenty declarations that share a name, spread over the very files the pool
lists — so the pool is satisfied by an answer that never asked about interface
satisfaction, and would keep being satisfied if `find_implementations` returned
nothing but the disambiguation list forever.

**After.** Anchored on the interface's own declaration — which is what the
task's `target` names, and what the probe now resolves to:

| arm | rows | provenance |
| --- | --- | --- |
| structural (`go` hidden) | **0** | `{"language":"go","semanticTier":"absent"}` |
| semantic | **18** | *(silent)* |

The 18: AsciiJSON, BSON, Data, HTML, IndentedJSON, JSON, JsonpJSON, MsgPack,
ProtoBuf, PureJSON, Reader, Redirect, SecureJSON, String, TOML, TestRender,
XML, YAML. `Context` is not among them.

`scripts/deriveSemanticOracles.ts` produces the same 18 names, from the rule
that a type satisfies `render.Render` iff its method set holds both
`Render(http.ResponseWriter) error` and
`WriteContentType(http.ResponseWriter)` — arrived at without g-mesh.

The oracle is now `mode: "judge"` and turns on two facts the disambiguation
list cannot supply:

- **`Context.Render` (context.go) is not an implementation.** It carries the
  name and not the method set — wrong signature, and no `WriteContentType` at
  all. It is *in* the structural answer and *absent* from the semantic one.
- **`render/render.go`'s own `var _ Render = (*T)(nil)` block is not the
  answer.** It lists 14, omitting BSON, MsgPack, PureJSON (and TestRender), and
  additionally names HTMLDebug and HTMLProduction, which implement
  `HTMLRender` — a different interface, measured separately at 0 rows
  structurally and 2 semantically. The rubric requires all three of BSON,
  MsgPack and PureJSON, which is what separates a method-set answer from one
  copied out of that block.

## gin — `gin-callers-writeheadernow-dispatch` (new)

gin's response writer is two layers: the exported `ResponseWriter` interface
and the unexported `responseWriter` struct behind it. Both declare
`WriteHeaderNow()`, and every call site in the repository is spelled the same.
`Context` holds one of each, side by side:

```go
writermem responseWriter
Writer    ResponseWriter
```

so `c.writermem.WriteHeaderNow()` hits the struct and
`c.Writer.WriteHeaderNow()` goes through the interface, and nothing at either
call site says which.

| anchor | structural | semantic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `ResponseWriter.WriteHeaderNow` (the interface method) | **0 rows**, `semanticTier: "absent"` | **10 caller symbols**, 3 files |
| `responseWriter.WriteHeaderNow` (the struct's) | **0 rows**, `semanticTier: "absent"` | **6 caller symbols**, 2 files |

The fact the structural arm lacks is the split itself: with `go` hidden the
answer to both questions is the same empty page, so there is nothing in it to
assign a call site to either group.

`deriveSemanticOracles.ts` reaches the same two sets from the source — 12 call
sites in 10 functions through the interface, 6 in 6 on the struct — by
resolving each receiver to its declaration (a struct field's declared type, a
method's own receiver, or a local bound by `w := ResponseWriter(writer)` /
`w := &responseWriter{...}` / a function literal's parameter). The rubric
requires both `Context.AbortWithStatus` and `Context.Render` on the interface
side, at least four of the six on the struct side including one of the three in
`gin.go`, and the deciding mechanism stated rather than a merged list.

## py-requests — the two new tasks, and the control that makes them mean something

Python's structural tier is not simply blind to receiver calls. A same-class
`self.x()` resolves without pyright, and that is the control this bucket needs:

| anchor | structural | semantic |
| --- | --- | --- |
| `PreparedRequest.prepare_url` — called as `self.prepare_url(...)` inside the same class | **1 row** | 1 row |

So a zero in the structural arm below is the absence of a specific kind of
resolution, not the arm answering nothing at all.

**`py-callers-prepare-two-classes`.** `Request.prepare` and
`PreparedRequest.prepare` share a name and do opposite things. Both call sites
of the mutating one are written `p = PreparedRequest()` then `p.prepare(...)` —
a plain local, no type at the call site.

| arm | rows |
| --- | --- |
| structural (`npx`, and so pyright, hidden) | **0**, `semanticTier: "absent"` |
| semantic | **2** — `Request.prepare` (models.py), `Session.prepare_request` (sessions.py) |

The derivation attributes each `<name>.prepare(` site to whichever class last
bound `<name>`, and finds the same two under `src/`, plus zero `src/` callers
for `Request.prepare` — whose only other appearances are tests and `>>>`
docstring examples.

**`py-callers-register-hook-mixin`.** `register_hook` is declared once, on
`RequestHooksMixin`, and reached three ways:

| arm | rows |
| --- | --- |
| structural | **0**, `semanticTier: "absent"` |
| semantic | **3** — `Request.__init__`, `PreparedRequest.prepare_hooks` (models.py), `HTTPDigestAuth.__call__` (auth.py) |

The third is the one the task turns on: `class HTTPDigestAuth(AuthBase)` does
not inherit the mixin at all. Its receiver is the method's own parameter,
annotated `def __call__(self, r: PreparedRequest)`, and that annotation is the
only thing in the file that connects the call to the mixin. The derivation
resolves each receiver either to its enclosing `class` statement (for `self`)
or to the enclosing `def`'s annotation, and prints the base list either way, so
the distinction is visible in the output rather than asserted.

### A draft the probe rejected

`find_callees(PreparedRequest.prepare)` looked like an obvious semantic task —
seven consecutive `self.prepare_*()` receiver calls. It returns the **same
seven rows in both arms**. Same-class `self` calls are resolved by the
structural tier, so it is a structural task and is not shipped as a semantic
one. `RequestEncodingMixin._encode_params` was a second draft and failed a
different way: **zero rows in both arms** (it is a `@staticmethod` behind
`@overload` stubs), so it distinguishes nothing.

## ripgrep — the explicit answer

**Rust's semantic tier is measurable by this corpus.** The premise that it is
not — "Rust's explicit `impl Trait for Type` syntax resolves trait dispatch
without a language server" — is half right, and the half it gets wrong is the
half the bucket needed.

Once `rustup` was hidden alongside `rust-analyzer`, so that the structural arm
was structural for the first time, the two arms separate cleanly, and they
separate **by tool, not by language**:

| call | structural | semantic | verdict |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| `find_implementations(Sink)` | 9 rows | 9 rows | structural |
| `find_implementations(Flag)` | 99 rows (`hasMore`) | 99 rows | structural |
| `find_references(SinkFinish)` | 15 rows / 7 files | 15 rows / 7 files | structural |
| `find_callers(flags::Flag::name_long)` | **0**, `semanticTier: "absent"` | **14 rows / 7 files** | **semantic** |
| `find_callers(flags::Flag::doc_category)` | **0**, `semanticTier: "absent"` | 3 rows / 2 files | semantic |
| `find_callers(sink::Sink::matched)` | **0**, `semanticTier: "absent"` | 3 rows / 2 files | **semantic** |
| `find_callers(sink::Sink::finish)` | **0**, `semanticTier: "absent"` | 3 rows / 2 files | semantic |

So the original premise holds for `find_implementations`: an `impl Trait for
Type` line names both sides, and a syntactic pass reads it — including across
crates, and including the two blanket impls. It does **not** hold for
`find_callers` on a trait method, where the receiver is a `&dyn Flag` trait
object or a generic `S: Sink`, nothing at the call site names a type, and the
structural arm returns an empty page that says so.

That is also why every existing ripgrep task read as structural: seven of the
eight use `find_implementations`, `find_references`, `find_definition`,
`get_dependencies`, `get_file_outline` or `search_code`. The eighth,
`rs-callers-sink-matched`, is a `find_callers` task and was mistagged for the
two instrument reasons above, not because of anything about Rust.

### `rs-callers-flag-name-long-dyn` (new)

`Flag::name_long` is declared 105 times: once as a required method on the
`Flag` trait, and 104 times as `impl Flag for <flag struct>` bodies that are
string literals and call nothing. Every caller reaches it through
`pub(super) const FLAGS: &[&dyn Flag]` or a `&dyn Flag` parameter.

The fact the structural arm lacks: **all of them**. Zero rows, with
`provenance {"language":"rust","semanticTier":"absent"}` on the empty page.
The semantic arm returns 14 caller symbols over 7 files.

The independent derivation finds 14 non-test call sites in 8 functions across
6 files, plus 7 test call sites in defs.rs's own `mod tests`. The rubric is
scoped to the non-test files and requires the `&dyn Flag` mechanism to be
stated, because the 104 impls are what a name-keyed answer returns instead.

One divergence worth recording, since it is the kind of thing this benchmark
exists to find: g-mesh's semantic arm returns 7 of the 8 non-test callers. It
misses `doc/help.rs`'s `generate_short_flag`, whose single call site sits
inside a `write!` macro invocation. The oracle asks for files rather than an
exact function set, so both the derivation and a correct agent answer remain
gradable.

### `rs-callers-sink-matched` (re-tagged, not new)

Its own call — `find_callers` on the `Sink` trait's `matched` — returns 0 rows
structurally and 3 semantically (the two blanket-impl forwards in `sink.rs`,
and `Core::sink_matched` in `searcher/core.rs`). Its oracle needs exactly those
files, so it is a semantic-tier task and was tagged `structural` only because
the arms were identical and the anchor never resolved.

Two changes came with the re-tag. Its `target.symbol` was a bare `"matched"` —
one of six declarations of that name in `sink.rs` alone — and is now
`sink::Sink::matched`, the declaration its own prompt describes. And two
factual errors in its rubric were corrected: the non-delegating call site is
`Core::sink_matched` (core.rs:522, calling `self.sink.matched(...)` at
core.rs:536), not `Core::matched` (core.rs:99), which only forwards to it; and
the rubric cited g-mesh's own `find_callers` as a source for its ground truth,
which GMB-160 forbids, so that clause now cites the source read directly.

## The bucket, and what it costs

Eight tasks, as targeted, three/three/two across the corpora:

| corpus | task | tool | structural → semantic |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| gin | `gin-find-impl-render` *(fixed)* | `find_implementations` | 0 → 18 |
| gin | `gin-callers-writeheadernow-dispatch` *(new)* | `find_callers` | 0 → 10 (and 0 → 6 for the struct's method) |
| gin | `gin-scenario-abort-callers` *(already tagged)* | `find_callers` | 0 → 6 |
| py-requests | `py-callers-prepare-two-classes` *(new)* | `find_callers` | 0 → 2 |
| py-requests | `py-callers-register-hook-mixin` *(new)* | `find_callers` | 0 → 3 |
| py-requests | `py-scenario-callers-httpadapter-send` *(already tagged)* | `find_callers` | 0 → 1 |
| ripgrep | `rs-callers-flag-name-long-dyn` *(new)* | `find_callers` | 0 → 14 |
| ripgrep | `rs-callers-sink-matched` *(re-tagged)* | `find_callers` | 0 → 3 |

A note on the shape of that table: seven of the eight are `find_callers`, and
the eighth is `find_implementations`. That is not a lack of imagination, it is
what the tiers are. GM-382's own scoping says the semantic tier contributes
*edges* — resolved receiver calls and `SUPERTYPE_OF` links — so only four tools
can depend on it at all, and of those four, `find_references` and
`find_callees` turned out to resolve structurally in every shape tried here
(Python's `self.x()` callees; Rust's `SinkFinish` references). A bucket that
looked more varied would be a bucket with structural tasks in it.
